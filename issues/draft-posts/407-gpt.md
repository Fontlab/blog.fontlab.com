## the old fonts are not dead; they’re just waiting in a folder

---
title: the old fonts are not dead; they’re just waiting in a folder
slug: the-old-fonts-are-not-dead
date: 2026-05-07
authors:
  - editorial
categories:
  - TransType 4
  - OpenType
  - typography
summary: Why a font converter becomes interesting again when old Type 1 libraries, legacy layouts, and web formats all collide.
---

Nothing gets old faster than a font format — until a client opens a twenty-year-old file and suddenly it is everybody’s problem again. Adobe ended authoring support for PostScript Type 1 fonts in updated Creative Cloud apps after January 2023, while Type Network notes that pre-OpenType documents can still hide replacement problems in spacing, kerning, line breaks, expert sets, and old naming conventions. OpenType took over because Type 1 could not keep up with bigger character sets, Unicode, and richer layout behavior; WOFF and then WOFF2 made that newer world practical on the web as well. citeturn10view1turn29view0turn28search1turn28search9

![Old Type 1 and OpenType font icons floating on a bright blue desktop](https://typenetwork.com/_next/image?q=75&url=https%3A%2F%2Fcms.typenetwork.com%2Fmedia%2Foriginal_images%2FType1_Banner.png&w=3840)

That banner is excellent because it does not pretend migration is romantic. It is just a blue desktop full of old font suitcases and old Adobe-style icons, which is exactly what the job feels like: a little absurd, a little archaeological, and absolutely real. TransType 4 becomes interesting in that light. Not as a shiny converter. As a rescue boat. The useful story is not “convert fonts.” It is “save the working history of documents before the next missing-font dialog starts eating them.” citeturn30view0turn29view0turn10view0

If I were writing this for the FontLab orbit, I would keep the angle narrow and practical: font conversion as preservation, not convenience. Designers do not need another sermon about future-proofing. They need a reminder that older files are full of small typographic traps, and that a careful conversion workflow is less like exporting and more like moving a library without scrambling the shelves. citeturn29view0turn10view1

## a width axis is not a squeeze command

---
title: a width axis is not a squeeze command
slug: a-width-axis-is-not-a-squeeze-command

authors:

summary: The best multilingual writing on variable fonts agrees on one thing: proper variation is design, not distortion.
---

Read enough German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, Ukrainian, Russian, Chinese, Japanese, and Korean writing on variable fonts and the cliché falls apart. Serious sources in all those languages keep returning to the same point: variable fonts are not just a flashy weight slider. They package multiple styles or axes into one OpenType file, help with responsive typography and web performance, and, more importantly, give designers finer control over width, slant, optical size, and motion. The details differ, but the pattern is strikingly consistent across the multilingual material. citeturn8view2turn35view0turn35view1turn35view2turn34view2turn34view0turn35view3turn34view3turn8view0turn8view1turn34view1

![A Chinese comparison showing crude horizontal squeezing versus a properly designed condensed width](https://blog.justfont.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/4.-UD-%E6%96%B0%E9%BB%91%E9%AB%94%E7%9A%84%E5%AE%98%E6%96%B9%E7%AA%84%E9%AB%94%E8%88%87%E6%9A%B4%E5%8A%9B%E5%A3%93%E7%AA%84%E6%AF%94%E8%BC%83jpeg.jpeg)

The justfont image says more than half the web’s variable-font hype ever did. The top line is the original. The lower left is brute-force squeezing. The lower right is the designer’s condensed version. Same word, same width target, very different result. The “violent” compression disturbs stroke logic and texture; the designed version keeps the character’s voice. That is the article. That is the whole article. And it points straight at FontLab 8: if a width axis matters, it matters because somebody drew and checked the intermediate decisions. citeturn8view0turn33view1

Underware’s writing on variable fonts is still the useful antidote to timid thinking. Their argument is not that variable fonts let you put Light and Black in one file. Their argument is that the format gets interesting when you stop treating old categories as sacred: time can be an axis, motion can be an axis, and the interface can become part of the typeface experience. Korean design-system notes around Roboto Flex land in a similarly practical place: slant, clarity, and fine-grained parameters are valuable because they let a designer build a better hierarchy, not because they make a software demo look busy. That gives FontLab 8 a very good editorial lane: not “look, sliders,” but “here is where deliberate variation stops being a trick and starts becoming typography.” citeturn31view0turn31view1turn34view1

## OpenType is where good manners live

---
title: OpenType is where good manners live
slug: opentype-is-where-good-manners-live
date: 2026-05-07
authors:
  - editorial
categories:
  - FontLab 8
  - OpenType

summary: Ligatures, small caps, script behavior, and feature tags are not extras. They are the quiet parts that keep text civil.
---

Good typography does a lot of work without announcing itself. OpenType’s layout model gives fonts tables and feature tags for substitution and positioning, so ligatures, small caps, numeral styles, contextual alternates, and script-specific forms can happen when they should. That is not decoration. It is basic social behavior for text. A font that knows when to substitute, join, or shift is simply a better citizen on the page. citeturn7search0turn7search4turn1search3turn1search12

![A comparison of false small caps, real small caps, and lowercase letters](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3b/Small_caps_2.png?utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original&utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org)

The Minion Pro comparison is wonderfully blunt. Fake small caps look like capital letters that lost a fight with the copy machine. Real small caps look designed. Proportion, weight, and color stay coherent. Once you see the A–B–C comparison, it becomes hard to unsee how often people settle for shrunken shouting. citeturn18view0turn22view0

That is why the smart FontLab 8 article here is not a tour of checkboxes. It is a piece about manners: Turkish forms that should not collide with careless substitutions, Arabic joining that must happen correctly, fractions that should feel intentional, and small caps that should not be fake souvenirs. Simon Cozens’s explanations of feature logic and global-script behavior pair beautifully with Microsoft’s feature registry for this kind of post. The real pitch is simple: OpenType features are UX for text. Users notice them most when they are missing. citeturn1search19turn7search19turn7search7turn1search9

## white space does the real drawing

---
title: white space does the real drawing
slug: white-space-does-the-real-drawing
date: 2026-05-07
authors:
  - editorial
categories:
  - FontLab 8
  - spacing
  - type design
summary: A short piece about counters, rhythm, spacing groups, and why the emptiest part of a glyph is often the most important.
---

Letterforms are made of black shapes, but type is judged by the white around and inside them. Adrian Frutiger’s famous line of thought — that the empty spaces are the crucial part of a typeface — still feels like the cleanest correction to beginner enthusiasm. People fall in love with terminals, serifs, and flamboyant tails. The page falls apart because the counters and sidebearings were treated as afterthoughts. citeturn7search3turn7search12turn13search14

Walter Tracy’s spacing logic remains useful precisely because it is unfancy. Start with a few key letters, think in groups, build rhythm before you chase exceptions, and do not confuse kerning with structural spacing. Research on spacing methods makes the same point in more formal language: fitting is not cosmetic repair after drawing; it is part of the drawing. That is a perfect FontLab 8 theme because it turns the software away from “how to kern fast” and toward the harder truth: if the default rhythm is wrong, you are decorating a problem. citeturn13search7turn13search1turn25search11

![A specimen image of Méridien by Adrian Frutiger](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a9/M%C3%A9ridien_sample.png?utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original&utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org)

The Méridien specimen is a good image for this because the giant **g** is less a flourish than a spacing lesson in public. The counters, the bowl, the internal air, the way the italic word *Lazy* changes the rhythm of the line — it all reads as proportion and interval before it reads as style. Optical sizes push the same idea further: text and display cuts are not vanity variations, but different answers to different spatial problems. citeturn19view0turn22view3turn26search0turn26search17turn26search20

## Vexy Lines and the useful lie of engraving

---
title: Vexy Lines and the useful lie of engraving
slug: vexy-lines-and-the-useful-lie-of-engraving
date: 2026-05-07
authors:
  - editorial
categories:
  - Vexy Lines
  - engraving
  - halftones
  - vector art
summary: Why the right story for Vexy Lines is reduction, not realism — and why old print tricks still feel new when vectors do the pretending.
---

Engraving is a useful lie. A hedcut looks old, sober, authoritative, a little expensive — even when it was made yesterday. That is why the style keeps working. The Wall Street Journal hedcut tradition explicitly leans on dots and hatch lines to imitate older engravings, and the whole trick succeeds because the image refuses full realism without giving up recognizability. It is edited likeness. Not likeness alone. citeturn20view1turn5search0

![A classic pen-and-ink hedcut portrait](https://www.hedcut.com/uploads/1/1/5/7/115791943/jobs_2.jpg)

Halftones do a related trick with different math. Screen-printing guides describe them as grids of dots translating grayscale into printable tonal values, with line frequency and angle deciding how much detail survives. Independent listings for Vexy Lines describe a tool built around that territory: linear, wave, halftone, text, and handmade fills, with vector export aimed at print-ready SVG or PDF. That is a promising combination because it frames the app less as “auto-trace, again” and more as an instrument for choosing what to keep and what to throw away. citeturn20view2turn27view0turn27view1

![A close-up screen-printed halftone portrait in metallic ink](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/2636/4748/files/IMG_5533_480x480.jpg?v=1680019384)

The two images make the editorial angle obvious. The hedcut portrait wins by turning skin into dots and cloth into hatch texture. The metallic halftone print wins by letting the grid stay visible. Vexy Lines should lean into that honesty. The fun is not that vectors can mimic a photograph perfectly. The fun is that they can stop at exactly the right point and let the method show. A good Vexy post, then, is not a feature roundup. It is a manifesto for tasteful loss. citeturn21view0turn21view2turn27view0
