2005 was a pivotal year for Fontlab Ltd.: FontLab Studio 5 shipped and became the
dominant professional font editor for the following decade, and the company acquired
Fontographer from Macromedia, reviving a tool dormant since the mid-1990s.

<!-- more -->

FontLab Studio 5 succeeded FontLab 4, which had introduced the first OpenType editing
capability in December 2001. Studio 5 refined and extended that foundation into the
de-facto standard for professional type design and font production.
Adobe, Apple, IBM, Microsoft, Monotype, Morisawa, and most major font foundries used it
for production work.
The fonts it helped produce include Calibri, Candara, Consolas, Myriad Pro, Palatino
nova, PT Sans, and many others in wide daily use.

In May 2005, Fontlab Ltd.
licensed Fontographer from Macromedia.
Fontographer had been the dominant font editor on the Macintosh through the late 1980s
and early 1990s — it introduced graphical Bézier font editing to a wide audience — but
development had stalled after Macromedia acquired it in 1995. Fontlab Ltd.
took the rights and resumed development.
Fontographer 5 eventually shipped in 2010.

Also in 2005, the company released FontFlasher for pixel font creation and FogLamp, a
utility for converting native Fontographer files into native FontLab files — a practical
bridge for studios moving between the two tools.

The OpenType editing work that began in FontLab 4 and matured in Studio 5 gave type
designers direct access to the feature code layer of OpenType fonts — ligatures,
alternate forms, positional variants, and script-specific rules — without requiring
separate tools or hand-editing binary tables.
That capability, combined with class-based kerning and visual TrueType hinting, made
Studio 5 the tool of record for a generation of professional type production.
