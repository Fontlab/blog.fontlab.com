On March 11, 2014, FontLab hosted a webinar on Roman capital letterforms and their
origins in flat-brush writing, presented by sign painter and type designer John Downer
and calligrapher Paul Herrera.

<!-- more -->

The session demonstrated how the structure of Imperial Roman capitals — the letterforms
that underpin most Western type design — can be traced to marks made by a flat brush on
a vertical surface. Downer used live brush demonstration to show how the tool’s geometry
generates the thick-thin stroke contrast, serif terminations, and proportional
relationships that define the classical Latin alphabet.

**John Downer** has worked as a journeyman sign painter for more than forty years.
He began designing typefaces thirty years ago, approaching letterform design through
professional hand lettering rather than historical models alone.
His typefaces include Brothers, Roxy, Iowan Old Style, and Vendetta — each carrying
structural decisions traceable to his sign-painting practice.

**Paul Herrera** trained under Reverend Edward M. Catich, beginning in 1967 as an
undergraduate. Catich, an inscription cutter and calligrapher, developed influential
theories on the brush origins of Roman capitals, documented in his 1968 book *The Origin
of the Serif*. Herrera worked alongside Catich as an inscription cutter and seminar
assistant until Catich’s death in 1979, then taught Catich’s classes at St.
Ambrose University through 1989. He also served on the faculty of five international
calligraphy conventions between 1981 and 1987, and continued inscription work for
Wichita State University and architectural clients over a forty-year career.

The webinar drew a direct line from historical stonecutting and brushwork to the
practical concerns of contemporary type design — how understanding the physical origins
of letterforms informs decisions about weight distribution, stroke modulation, and serif
construction.
