In February 2014, FontLab hosted a live webinar with Matthew Carter, one of the most consequential type designers of the digital era.

Matthew Carter’s career spans the full arc of type technology — from cutting punches for metal type at the Enschedé foundry in the 1950s, through the phototypesetting era, and into digital. He co-founded Bitstream, the first digital type foundry, in 1981.

His typefaces are embedded in everyday reading. Bell Centennial, commissioned by AT&T in the late 1970s, was engineered for phone book constraints — small size, poor paper, high-speed presses — and remained in use for decades. Verdana and Georgia, commissioned by Microsoft in the mid-1990s, were designed from the outset for low-resolution screen rendering, anticipating the pixel grid constraints that would define web typography for years. Snell Roundhand brought a copperplate script into digital form. Carter Sans balanced legibility with humanist warmth. Galliard drew on the sixteenth-century types of Robert Granjon.

The webinar was an interview and Q&A covering Carter’s working methods, his views on the relationship between technical constraints and design decisions, and the transition from metal type to digital font production.
