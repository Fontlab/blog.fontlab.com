Digital formats do not die of natural causes. They are usually smothered in their sleep by the companies that invented them.

PostScript Type 1 arrived in 1985 and became the absolute standard for professional print. Foundries built businesses on it. Studios curated libraries of it. Then OpenType arrived, with proper Unicode and bigger character sets, and the slow handover began. The handover stopped being slow in early 2023, when Adobe pulled the rug. Type 1 fonts that had loaded into Illustrator yesterday no longer loaded into Illustrator today. Apple quietly mirrored the move on macOS. A whole class of files was bricked overnight.

The technical reasons Type 1 lost are well rehearsed. Each file maxed out at 256 glyphs, which meant a single typeface family was usually scattered across half a dozen files — one for the basic set, one for expert fractions, one for small caps. Naming conventions were chaotic. Cross-platform behaviour was unreliable. OpenType fixed all of that and added a proper layout engine on top.

What got lost in the format change was hand-tuned data. Years of careful kerning. Style links that lined up with how a designer actually used the family. Naming that matched the printed specimen. Throwing the fonts away meant throwing that work away too.

This is the niche TransType 4 has occupied since 2013. Drag in the Type 1 files; out come modern OpenType OTFs with the family glued back together, the names sane, the kerning preserved as far as the format gap allows. It is not a font editor. It is a moving van. The point is to relocate the typeface without scrambling the shelves.

If a font is worth keeping, it is worth converting carefully. The graveyard is full of files that nobody bothered to move.
