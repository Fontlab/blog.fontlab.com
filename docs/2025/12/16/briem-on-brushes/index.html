    Briem on brushes and broad-pen logic

    Gunnlaugur SE Briem has spent a career arguing that you cannot draw a serious typeface without understanding how a broad-edged pen makes letters. Briem’s tutorial on brushes is the cleanest version of that argument you will find. (Note: Unlike other FontLab TV episodes like this playlist, there is no video for this tutorial—it is purely a written masterclass.)

    What it covers¶

    Why broad-pen first. Roman letters were drawn with broad-edged tools. The contrast distribution in any text serif — thick verticals, thin horizontals, the diagonal axis on o — is a direct fossil of the pen angle. Without this you are reverse-engineering shapes you cannot defend.

    The pen angle. Hold a flat-edged tool at roughly 30° from the baseline and the contrast falls out automatically: thicks on the down-strokes, thins on the up- and horizontal-strokes. The tutorial shows the same letter drawn with the pen at 0°, 30°, and 45° — the third one is what produces the typical Italic axis.

    Brush vs broad-edge. A pointed brush adds pressure as a third variable. Same axis, but with continuous contrast modulation rather than the on/off contrast of a flat pen. Brush letters explain almost every italic and a lot of script type.

    Mapping calligraphy onto digital drawing. Briem’s claim is not that you should literally draw with a brush in FontLab. It is that the curves you draw with the pen tool should be the curves a flat-edged pen would have produced. The tutorial shows side-by-side comparisons of curves drawn “by feel” vs curves drawn after twenty minutes with a pen.

    Why it matters¶

    Most “stiff” letters come from designers who have never held a broad-edged pen. This tutorial is a fix for that — and a useful re-read every six months.
